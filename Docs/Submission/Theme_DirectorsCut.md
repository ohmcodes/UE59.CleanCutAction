# Tell us about your take on the theme and how it influenced your game?

**Theme:** Director's Cut

---

We took "Director's Cut" literally and put the player on the film set, working under the director. In the movies, the director's cut is the version of the film the director wanted to make. In *Clean! Cut! Action!*, it's the version Marlowe Finch is trying to get on camera, and you're the production assistant who has to make it happen.

The theme shaped the whole game around one question: **what does it take, behind the camera, to get the shot the director wants?**

**Phase 1: Clean (the set has to be perfect).** A director's cut starts with a set that matches the director's vision exactly. Last night's wrap party trashed the two-floor cafe set, and Marlowe wants every cup, chair and croissant back on its mark before the cameras roll. Every prop has a glowing tape-mark silhouette, which is how real sets mark where things go between takes. Sloppy work breaks props and costs points, because a picky director notices everything.

**Phase 2: Action (the scene has to land).** Once the set is clean, actors Dash and Juniper shoot their rainy first-date scene, and Marlowe calls the cues through his megaphone: rain, traffic, thunder, steam, sunset, wind, the boom mic, the umbrella toss. You run between practical-effect stations on both floors and fire each one on time. You also have to stay out of the camera's frame, which is drawn on the floor as a cone. Every cue is graded Perfect, Good or Late, and a take meter fills or drains. Let the take meter run dry and Marlowe yells "CUT! Reset!", and the act restarts as the next take. The game never ends on a failed take, because on a film set you keep shooting until you get it.

**The title is a shoot in three words.** *Clean* is the set dressing, *Cut* is the director stopping a bad take, and *Action* is the scene itself. The theme also decided who talks to you: Marlowe is the tutorial, the score screen and the comic relief. Each result comes with one of his lines, so the director judges every take, not just the final one.

**The story behind the cut.** Dash and Juniper are real-life exes who can barely look at each other, and the scripted scene is their characters' first date. The director's cut that ends up on film isn't the one Marlowe scripted. When you nail a cue, the actors drift off the script and start talking to each other for real ("That's not the line." "It's my line now."), and Marlowe whispers "Keep rolling, keep rolling...". At the wrap, the actors ask for retakes and Marlowe refuses: "That WAS the take! That's the Director's Cut!" The best take comes from the crew getting the details right, not from the director's script.

**In short:** most games about directing put you in the director's chair. We put you under it, as the person who makes the director's cut possible.
